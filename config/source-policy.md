# Source and Verification Policy

## Principle

The brief should make it easy to answer: "How do we know this?"

A source can establish that someone made a claim without establishing that the claim is true.

## Source hierarchy

### Primary material

Prefer primary material when it directly answers the question:

- Government statements, ministries, legislatures, election commissions
- Treaties, legal filings, court judgments, sanctions notices, executive orders
- Official military releases and imagery, with appropriate skepticism
- International organizations and monitoring bodies
- Transcripts, speeches, press conferences, and published policy documents
- Public datasets and official economic releases

Primary does not mean unbiased. It means close to the underlying act or statement.

### High-quality reporting

Use reputable wire services and established reporting organizations for independent reporting, especially when they have reporters on the ground, document review, direct interviews, or transparent sourcing.

### Specialist analysis

Use credible research institutes, domain experts, academic work, specialist defense/economic reporting, and transparent OSINT for context or technical interpretation.

Specialist analysis should not silently replace factual verification.

### Discovery-only material

Treat these as leads, not final evidence:

- Search-result snippets
- Social-media reposts
- Aggregators
- Unsourced maps or graphics
- AI-generated summaries
- Wikipedia for current-event claims

Follow the lead to the underlying source.

## Independence

Two articles are not independent confirmation when both rely on:

- the same government statement;
- the same unnamed official;
- the same wire story;
- the same viral video;
- the same think-tank estimate.

Record common provenance when you can identify it.

## Conflict and intelligence claims

For battlefield, intelligence, covert-action, and casualty claims:

1. State who is making the claim.
2. State whether it is independently corroborated.
3. Prefer observable evidence when available.
4. Treat intelligence assessments as assessments unless supporting evidence is public.
5. Avoid confident causal attribution when only capability or motive is known.

## Verification threshold

For a major factual claim, seek either:

- a directly relevant primary source plus credible independent reporting; or
- two genuinely independent credible sources.

This is a default, not a mechanical rule. Some events are established by one authoritative primary record; some cannot yet be independently verified. In those cases, preserve the limitation explicitly.

## Quotes and numbers

- Verify quotations against the transcript or a reliable direct report when possible.
- Check units, currencies, dates, and timezones.
- Distinguish announced commitments from delivered capability.
- Distinguish nominal spending from real resources when relevant.
- Do not report precision that the source does not justify.

## Corrections and disagreement

When credible sources conflict:

- identify exactly what they disagree about;
- describe the strongest evidence available to each side;
- avoid averaging incompatible claims;
- use calibrated language such as "confirmed," "supported," "plausible," "unclear," or "unsupported."

## Citation behavior

Every major section of the final brief should contain direct links to the sources most useful for checking the claims.

Do not dump a giant undifferentiated bibliography at the end as a substitute for claim-level sourcing.
