## Cross-cutting judgments

### The Middle East fight now has competing scoreboards

The Houthi strike shows that geographic reach remains available to the Iran-aligned network even as CENTCOM claims economic control at Hormuz. Both can be true: the United States can improve commercial transit while adversaries shift political pressure toward Saudi cities and infrastructure. View the Riyadh attack as a challenge to the coalition's political endurance, not proof that the Hormuz campaign failed.

CENTCOM's decision to publish categorical numbers invites external measurement. The one-billion-barrel figure and zero-export claim should become recurring audit points. If the command later supplies definitions that reconcile with commercial tracking, the figures strengthen deterrence and blockade credibility. If it does not, the rhetoric risks outrunning observable market conditions.

### Security commitments are being announced earlier than they become real

The Ukraine and Greenland developments have the same editorial trap: policy authorization is easy to mistake for implemented capacity. Ukraine's notice authorizes a possible sale and identifies a technical direction, but quantities, contractors, and delivery are unknown. The Greenland announcement describes a deal expected to be signed, but the text and ratification path remain outstanding. In both cases, the decisive evidence will be downstream: contracts and delivery schedules for Ukraine; clauses, annexes, and parliamentary approval for Greenland.

### The Caribbean campaign's information deficit grows with its body count

One more strike is not a strategic turning point. A total of at least 231 deaths across 69 strikes is. The administration's armed-conflict framing moves conduct that would ordinarily require interdiction, evidence, and adjudication into a targeting model. The public record proves repeated lethal action, not the identities or cargoes of individual targets. That distinction should remain central.
